# Rabbit digestion in hot and cold weather: what the evidence supports

*Heat is the best-studied weather threat to rabbit digestion. Betaine has more rabbit trials than any other heat additive, with gut-marker benefits but mixed growth results. Common electrolyte doses have no trials behind them, and cold-weather evidence is thin.*

## Key findings

- Heat makes rabbits eat less: in 80 does kept under a 24–31 °C summer cycle, feed intake fell 9.4% while water intake did not rise [1].
- Betaine is the most-tested heat additive (five controlled trials, four under heat, 539 farmed rabbits): every trial that measured the gut reported better gut markers, but growth improved in one 150-rabbit trial and not in a near-identical 100-rabbit trial [2, 3, 4, 5, 6].
- Vitamin C helped heat-stressed farmed rabbits at feed doses from 5 to 1,000 mg/kg, but we found no rabbit study comparing doses [4, 7, 8, 9].
- Widely quoted potassium chloride and sodium bicarbonate doses for rabbit drinking water trace to Chinese-language trade notes, not trials [10].
- The only cold-weather trial we found was on a farm: warm drinking water in a winter barn cut late diarrhoea (2 of 59 vs 14 of 60 rabbits) but not overall growth [11].
- Chamomile and valerian extracts relax isolated rabbit gut muscle, but no study has tested whether they slow digestion in live rabbits [12, 13, 14].

## The question

Many owners worry that hot spells, cold snaps or the change of season can tip a rabbit into gut stasis, a dangerous slowdown of the digestive tract. Electrolytes, "cooling" supplements, probiotics and herbal blends are sold for these moments. We asked what the published evidence shows, and which of these practices have rabbit data behind them.

## How we researched it

This review was produced by MSRA's AI-powered research system. It re-uses our September 2026 literature runs on small-mammal heat stress and gut health, narrowed to rabbits, plus about ten new PubMed searches on cold weather, season and gut stasis in pet rabbits. Searches used PubMed and Europe PMC; reviews were used to find primary trials, which were then cited directly.

In the earlier runs, the heat-stress search (16 queries) surfaced about 540 records; about 200 were screened at abstract level and about 30 read in depth. The vitamin C and betaine search screened about 150 records (15 in depth), the gut-motility search about 110 (about 35 in depth), and a probiotic, fibre and gut-microbiology sweep retrieved 725 records from 15 queries. Every citation was re-checked against PubMed and every quoted number against the study's abstract or full text; where our earlier notes disagreed with a paper, we followed the paper.

## Why weather matters to a rabbit's gut

Rabbits ferment fibre in a large hindgut and depend on a steady flow of food through it. A 2024 review of digestive disease in farmed rabbits describes two routes to trouble: an "overload" of easily fermented starch or protein reaching the caecum, and a "chymus jam", where stress or too little fibre slows the hindgut and caecal fermentation goes wrong [15]. It is a working model from production rabbits, not a proven mechanism in pets.

Heat is the clearest weather stressor. Rabbits lack functional sweat glands [2] and lose heat through their ears and faster breathing [16]. In climate-chamber tests on adult rabbits, 32.2 °C was tolerated for seven hours, but at 37.8 °C body temperature reached the 41.7 °C safety limit within 80 minutes; at 34.5 °C, rabbits lasted 323 minutes on average in dry air but only 81–119 minutes in humid air [17]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) notes that feed intake falls above about 30 °C, that water intake relative to feed rises from about 1.8 to over 3.1 times at 30 °C, and that rabbits drink less from water colder than 10 °C or warmer than 25 °C [16]. Under moderate heat, though, does ate 9.4% less and did not drink more [1].

One caution applies throughout: almost all of this research is in young meat rabbits on farms, often in hot climates, measured on production outcomes. Pet rabbits are older, eat hay-based diets and often live indoors, and their faecal bacteria differ from those of commercial, laboratory and shelter rabbits [18].

**Betaine** (trimethylglycine, a natural compound found in beet) is the most-tested heat additive in rabbits, but the honest reading is narrower than "betaine works". Every trial that measured the gut reported better gut markers. Growth is another matter: one research group ran two near-identical summer trials, and 1.5 g/kg improved growth in 150 rabbits [2] while no dose changed growth or health in 100 rabbits [3]. The Egyptian trial had no unstressed comparison group, and all four additives helped [4]. Three of the five trials come from one group. None was in adult pet rabbits, none measured droppings, appetite or stasis, and none tested betaine in rabbit drinking water; in broilers, betaine in drinking water did nothing under heat [20].

**Vitamin C** has positive farm trials in feed and in water, measured on growth and blood markers rather than gut function in pets.

**Electrolytes** are the weakest link. A widely cited 2022 review recommends potassium chloride (0.3–0.5%), sodium bicarbonate (0.1–0.2%) and ammonium chloride (2%) in drinking water [10]. Its references for these doses lead to Chinese-language reviews and one-page trade notes, and it recommends both an alkalinising and an acidifying salt for the same problem. The one PubMed-indexed controlled rabbit study of bicarbonate in water gives neither concentration nor group sizes in its abstract [19]. Changing the water can backfire: acidified water reduced drinking and growth in 240 young rabbits [21], and overly salty water is drunk less [16].

**Cool water** has one small rabbit trial: it lowered afternoon body temperature slightly but did not improve growth [22].

## Cold and seasonal change

Evidence here is thin and almost entirely from farms. EFSA concludes that in farmed rabbits cold stress mainly threatens newborn kits, while heat is the main thermal problem after weaning. It lists draughts and low temperature among hazards for digestive disorders, and frozen water as a cause of prolonged thirst [16].

The only cold-weather intervention trial we found used 180 newly weaned rabbits in a poorly insulated barn averaging 7.8 °C. Rabbits given warm water (35.5 °C) instead of cold water (5.8 °C) grew better in the first 12 days only. Diarrhoea did not differ for 24 days, but in the final 12 days it affected 2 of 59 warm-water rabbits against 14 of 60 cold-water rabbits [11]: one farm trial in young meat rabbits (grade B).

In 100 rabbits fed for 8 weeks in summer or winter, growth and feed intake were lower in summer; feed additives (organic selenium, a prebiotic, a bacterial probiotic or live yeast) improved intake and growth overall [23]. On one farm, 17 rabbits per season were heavier in winter and had more inflammatory and oxidative-stress markers and different gut bacteria in summer [24], though feeding times also differed between seasons.

Body temperature matters once a rabbit is ill. Among 117 pet rabbits seen for reduced eating and droppings, those hypothermic on arrival (36.6 °C or lower) were 5 times as likely to die or be euthanised [25]; among 316 hospitalised pet rabbits, each 1 °C drop in admission temperature doubled the odds of death [26]. This is about a sick rabbit's body temperature, not cold weather as a cause.

We found no study of whether gut stasis in pet rabbits is more common in any season, and no PubMed-indexed study linking moulting to stasis, despite how often that link is repeated.

## Everyday digestive support: fibre, starch, live yeast and probiotics

**Fibre** has the strongest evidence. A review of trials in growing rabbits recommends at least 18% indigestible fibre (ADF) and over 5% lignin [27]. When fibre was progressively replaced by starch, caecal fermentation fell, caecal pH and ammonia rose, and diarrhoea deaths increased at the lowest fibre level [28]. In 32 rabbits on four common pet diets, every muesli-fed group picked out favoured pieces and ate less hay and drank less water [29].

**Prebiotics** give mixed results. In 348 early-weaned rabbits, 4% inulin gave the lowest illness rate and better caecal fermentation, while 0.3% mannan-oligosaccharide lowered cellulose digestion; deaths did not differ, and benefits faded once the additives stopped [30].

**Live yeast and probiotics** have farm data but almost no pet-rabbit data. The only controlled pet-rabbit study, a double-blind crossover in 6 healthy rabbits, found that neither a live yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) nor *Enterococcus faecium* changed body weight or faecal weight or size; the only effect was more *E. faecium* in the droppings [31]. It is small and underpowered, but it is all there is. In farm rabbits, yeast plus mannan-oligosaccharide beat either alone (32 rabbits) [32]; *Bacillus subtilis* alone did no better than control while *Lactobacillus acidophilus* helped (64 rabbits) [33]; and across three 30-rabbit trials, *L. acidophilus* did not improve growth, faecal score, caecal pH or gut bacteria, though one trial reported less illness and fewer deaths [34]. Pet rabbits recovered from stasis carried more *Clostridium* and Enterobacteriaceae than healthy rabbits (22 vs 21), in an industry-funded study that cannot show cause and effect [35].

## Herbs that slow the gut

Many "calming" and digestive blends contain chamomile, valerian or lemon balm. In isolated rabbit jejunum, chamomile extract reduced spontaneous contractions at 0.003–0.013 mg/mL, acting like the calcium-channel-blocking drug verapamil [12]. An extract of *Valeriana hardwickii*, an Asian relative of common valerian, relaxed rabbit jejunum at 0.01–1 mg/mL [13]. Lemon balm extract mostly relaxed sheep gut tissue, in a study with industry co-authors [36], and Roman chamomile relaxed guinea-pig, rat and human gut tissue [37]. All of this is grade C.

Tissue results do not translate directly to live animals: a valerian extract that relaxed rabbit gut muscle in the laboratory sped up intestinal transit in mice [14]. No study has measured gut transit, droppings or appetite in live rabbits given any of these herbs, so the concern raised by the "chymus jam" model [15] is reasonable but unproven.

## What the evidence does not show

- No trial of any heat or cold supplement in pet rabbits.
- No controlled trial of potassium chloride, sodium bicarbonate or electrolyte solutions in rabbit drinking water at the commonly quoted doses.
- No evidence that betaine, vitamin C or any probiotic prevents gut stasis, and no randomised probiotic trial in pet rabbits measuring stasis, appetite or droppings.
- No study tying season or moulting to stasis risk in pet rabbits.
- No easy fix once the gut slows: in a blinded crossover in 10 healthy rabbits, four prokinetic drugs (metoclopramide, cisapride, pyridostigmine, capromorelin) did not change droppings or food intake over 72 hours [38].

## What this means for rabbit owners

This is a summary of research, not veterinary advice. Speak to a rabbit-experienced vet about your own animal.

- **Keep the diet steady and fibre-rich all year.** Hay-based diets have the strongest gut-health evidence; muesli-style mixes reduce hay and water intake [27, 29].
- **In hot weather, manage the environment first.** Provide shade and airflow and aim to keep rabbits below about 30 °C; humidity makes heat more dangerous [16, 17]. Expect appetite to dip, and watch eating and droppings closely [1].
- **Offer plain, fresh water at a comfortable temperature.** Rabbits drink less from water above 25 °C or below 10 °C [16]. If you try a water additive, keep plain water alongside, because additives can reduce drinking [21].
- **Treat betaine and vitamin C as unproven for pets.** The data come from farmed rabbits given them in feed; ask your vet first.
- **In cold weather, keep water unfrozen and shelter rabbits from draughts** [11, 16].
- **Be cautious with herbal "calming" or digestive blends.** Their effect on digestion in live rabbits is untested [12, 13].
- **Check your rabbit's rear end daily, especially when flies are about.** In a study of 6,349 pet rabbits in England, flystrike was the most common recorded cause of death, and soiled fur predisposes to it [39].
- **A rabbit that stops eating or stops passing droppings needs a vet promptly.** Anorexia and ileus (gut stasis) were among the commonest recorded causes of death in that study [39], the outlook depends on how early treatment starts [40], and a sick rabbit that is also cold is at higher risk [25, 26].
