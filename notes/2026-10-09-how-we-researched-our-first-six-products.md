Most pet supplements start from an ingredient someone already sells. Ours start from a problem owners describe in their own words: a rabbit that stops eating when the season turns, a parrot that plucks itself bare, a tortoise slow to feed after brumation.

This note explains how we went from those problems to six formulas, the numbers behind them, and where the evidence stops.

## Start from what owners write

Before any literature search, we read what owners actually say in reviews, forums and vet threads, and ranked problems by how deep the pain is and how poorly it is served today. Only then did we ask what the science says.

## Read the literature, then read it again

Our AI-powered research system searches and cross-checks the peer-reviewed literature, which makes it practical to review in days what would take a traditional team months. Our team reviewed every ingredient, dose and claim.

For the first six products:

| Step | Count |
|---|---:|
| PubMed-indexed studies in the evidence base | 226 |
| Candidate ingredients evaluated | 187 |
| Used in a final formula | 75 |
| Held as runners-up | 35 |
| Rejected, each with a written reason | 77 |

Each product was researched twice, independently. The second pass ran under a stricter rule: every dose needs a citation. It changed four of the six formulas. Where no evidence existed, the record says "not found" rather than filling the gap.

## Safety is the first filter

An ingredient that is popular in dog or human products can be wrong for a rabbit or a bird. Toxicity for the target species is checked before efficacy, and an ingredient that fails it never reaches the formula.

## Two findings we did not expect

**Calming herbs and the rabbit gut.** Chamomile and valerian are common in small-animal calming treats. In published tissue studies, both relax the muscle of the rabbit intestine [1] [2]. For an animal whose most feared emergency is a gut that slows down, that is the wrong direction. Settle contains no calming herbs.

**Fibre and feather pecking.** The best-evidenced dietary lever for feather pecking in birds turned out not to be a calming ingredient at all, but insoluble fibre. In a controlled study of laying hens, raising dietary insoluble fibre cut severe feather pecking from 0.78 to 0.12 pecks per 30 minutes [3]. PlumaCalm is built around it.

## Where the evidence stops

- The studies we cite tested ingredients or similar formulas, not our finished products.
- Some evidence comes from a different species. The fibre result is from hens. The calming peptide in Settle, PlumaCalm and CalmCat has a controlled trial in cats [4], but has not been studied in rabbits or parrots.
- Our in-house research facility tests raw materials and finished products for identity, potency and contaminants, and measures stability across shelf life. Animal studies of our finished products are planned with partner facilities.

We say this on every product page too, and we will update this note if new evidence changes the picture.

## What we will publish here

Research notes like this one, reading lists in the [research library](../library/studies.csv), and an explanation whenever a formula changes.
