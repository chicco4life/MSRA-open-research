# Preventing urinary blockages in cats: what the evidence supports

*Feeding wet food is the best-supported way to keep a cat's urine dilute, and diet can dissolve struvite stones. After a blockage, most drugs tested, including the widely prescribed prazosin, have not reduced re-blocking in controlled trials.*

[Sources used in this project](sources/)

## Key findings

- Wet food is the most effective way to raise a cat's water intake: in a randomized trial of 36 healthy cats, it nearly doubled total water intake and more than doubled urine output compared with dry food [1, 2].
- Diets with added salt or potassium chloride also make cats drink more and lower calculated stone risk, and a high-salt diet caused no high blood pressure or heart damage in healthy older cats over two years [3, 4, 5].
- Water fountains have not been shown to make urine more dilute; in a crossover trial of 14 cats, intake was the same from still, circulating and free-falling bowls [6, 7].
- A suitable therapeutic diet usually dissolves struvite bladder stones within about two to four weeks, but stones of other minerals do not dissolve [8, 9].
- About one in four male cats re-blocks within 30 days of being unblocked, and prazosin, the drug most often prescribed to prevent this, did not reduce re-blocking in two randomized trials [10, 11, 12].
- Meloxicam and bladder infusions of pentosan polysulfate or lidocaine also failed in randomized trials; one 2026 trial of the anti-anxiety drug lorazepam (no re-blocks versus 15.7% on placebo) is promising but unreplicated [13, 14, 15, 16].

## The question

Feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD) is an umbrella term for conditions that make a cat strain, urinate often, pass blood or urinate outside the litter box. The main causes are feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC, bladder inflammation with no identifiable cause), bladder stones, urethral obstruction and infection [17]; the most common stones are struvite and calcium oxalate [18]. In male cats the narrow urethra can be blocked by a plug or stone, a life-threatening emergency.

We asked two questions. What prevents FLUTD, in particular by getting cats to take in more water? And once a cat has had a blockage or a cystitis flare, which drugs, diets or procedures make a repeat less likely?

## How we researched it

We ran two literature reviews with MSRA's AI-powered research system. Both searched EuropePMC and PubMed and followed PRISMA 2020 reporting. Every statement in the underlying reports was fact-checked against the source papers, and for this summary we re-checked every cited study against PubMed.

Review 1 was a rapid review. In Review 2, 15 records that two independent screening passes could not settle were set aside rather than included, and surgery (such as perineal urethrostomy) and environmental changes on their own were outside its scope. The studies were too different to pool in a meta-analysis, so for each finding we state the study type and size (RCT means randomized controlled trial).

## Preventing FLUTD: water, moisture and diet

Dilute urine carries fewer of the minerals that form crystals and stones, and a veterinary nutrition review concludes that increasing water intake is essential whatever the stone type [19]. But cats are light drinkers: a 2026 scoping review of 32 studies found intakes of 22.8 to 51.2 mL per kg of body weight per day, often below the recommended 50 to 60 mL/kg. Diet format (wet or dry) was the strongest influence on intake, and water source had no reliable effect [20].

| Intervention | Evidence | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Wet vs dry food | Randomized 2×2 feeding trial, 36 healthy cats (9 per diet) [1] | Total water intake 54.3 vs 28.0 mL/kg/day; urine 34.0 vs 15.5 mL/kg/day; urine specific gravity (USG) 1.020 vs 1.051 |
| Food moisture from 3% to 82% | Crossover trial, 8 healthy cats [2] | Water 293 vs 109.2 mL/day; urine 228.2 vs 61.5 mL/day; USG 1.028 vs 1.064 (dry food with water added: 1.043) |
| 73% vs 6% moisture | Crossover trial, 6 healthy cats [21] | Calcium oxalate supersaturation 1.14 vs 2.29; bowl drinking fell but total intake rose; no clear struvite effect |
| Added salt (four levels) | Four diets fed in turn, 13 healthy cats [3] | Water intake 24.5 to 36.7 mL/kg/day; urine 10.9 to 19.6 mL/kg/day; calcium oxalate supersaturation 3.39 to 1.64; struvite 0.81 to 0.16 |
| Added potassium chloride | Crossover trial, 17 healthy adult cats [22]; three trials in senior cats, 9 to 12 each [4] | Adults: urine 11.2 to 16.2 mL/kg/day, calcium oxalate supersaturation 3.24 to 2.59. Seniors: urine volume up 25% to 71%, supersaturation 3.54 to 2.90 |
| Thickened water (1% methylcellulose) | Crossover trial, 12 healthy cats [23] | Intake up 25% at 28 days; USG 1.046 vs 1.053 |
| Nutrient-enriched water alongside dry food | Controlled trial, 18 healthy cats [24] | Urine output 15.2 vs 10.3 mL/kg/day; lower USG |
| Bowl type: still, circulating, free-falling | Randomized crossover, 14 laboratory cats [6] | Intake 27.5, 26.3 and 26.1 mL/kg/day (P = 0.942); urine volume and USG unchanged |
| Fountain vs bowl | Crossover, 12 healthy pet cats [7] | Measured intake 31.6 vs 22.9 mL/kg/day, but urine no more dilute; the fountain lost about four times more water to evaporation |

Supersaturation (RSS) estimates how likely urine is to form crystals of a mineral; below 1, crystals should not form. The pattern is consistent: water delivered in food works better than water the cat must choose to drink.

**Clinical outcomes are much thinner.** Almost all these trials measured urine in small groups of healthy research cats over days to weeks. The most direct clinical evidence is a 1999 prospective study of 54 client-owned cats with idiopathic cystitis fed the canned or dry version of the same diet: signs did not return within 12 months in 16 of 18 cats on canned food versus 17 of 28 on dry (P < 0.05); 8 cats were lost to follow-up and assignment was not described as randomized [25]. A 2025 systematic review rated the evidence for therapeutic urinary diets and for more dietary moisture in FIC as good [26].

**Struvite needs more than dilution.** Struvite depends heavily on urine acidity: in one dissolution study, urine pH fell from 7.0 to 6.0 in cats whose stones dissolved, while urine concentration did not change [27]. Acidifying diets control struvite but may be a risk factor for calcium oxalate [28], whereas dilution lowered supersaturation for both minerals in the wet-food and salt trials [1, 3].

**Is extra salt safe?** In a 6-month randomized trial in 24 mature cats, a high-salt diet did not harm blood pressure, kidney values or bone mineral, or raise urinary calcium, including in a small subgroup with borderline-high creatinine [29]. A 2-year randomized, blinded trial in 20 healthy cats (mean age 10.1 years) found no high blood pressure and no change in heart structure or function beyond one minor measurement judged a chance finding [5]. In an 8-cat study, urinary calcium concentration held steady, though daily calcium excretion rose from 0.62 to 1.05 mg/kg; calcium oxalate supersaturation was unchanged [30]. These were healthy cats; the results do not extend to cats with heart or kidney disease [5]. Added potassium chloride was well tolerated in senior cats [4].

## Reducing recurrence after a blockage

Recurrence is common. In a 192-cat retrospective study, 10.9% of cats re-blocked within 24 hours of catheter removal and 23.6% (37 of 157 followed up) within 30 days [10]. In a 65-cat randomized trial, 16 cats (25%) re-blocked within 30 days: 10 while still in hospital, a median of 6 hours after the catheter came out, and 6 after going home, a median of 4.5 days later [11].

| Intervention | Evidence | Result | Our reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prazosin 0.5 mg twice daily for 7 days | RCT, 65 cats [11] | Re-blocked: 30% vs 18% on placebo (P = 0.27) | No benefit |
| Prazosin 0.25 mg twice daily for 1 month | RCT, 47 cats [12] | 15% vs 17% at 1 month; 37% vs 31% at 6 months | No benefit |
| Prazosin vs no prazosin | Retrospective, 388 cats [31] | 24% vs 13% re-blocked within 14 days | Possible harm, likely confounded |
| Meloxicam (anti-inflammatory) | RCT, 37 cats [13] | 22.2% vs 26.3% at 7 days; 33.3% vs 31.5% at 3 months | No benefit at the low dose used |
| Meloxicam added to phenoxybenzamine and alprazolam | RCT, 51 cats [32] | Odds ratio 0.63 (95% CI 0.13 to 2.97) for 6-month re-blocking | Inconclusive |
| Pentosan polysulfate infused into the bladder | RCT, 35 cats [14] | 16.7% vs 17.6% | No benefit |
| Glycosaminoglycans infused into the bladder | Pilot RCT, 16 cats [33] | 0 of 9 vs 3 of 7 at 7 days (P = 0.06) | Inconclusive; needs a larger trial |
| Lidocaine and bicarbonate infused into the bladder | RCT, 26 cats [15] | 58% vs 57% over 2 months | No benefit |
| Lorazepam (anti-anxiety) for 1 month | RCT, 80 cats, 72 completed [16] | No re-blocks with lorazepam vs 5 cats (15.7%) on placebo | Promising; one trial, read from its abstract; ataxia in 13% |
| Atracurium flushed into the urethra during unblocking | RCT, 45 cats [34] | Plug cleared at first attempt in 64% vs 15% | Helps the procedure, not long-term recurrence |
| Routine antibiotics | Retrospective study and RCT analysis [10, 32] | No association with re-blocking | Use when a urine culture is positive |

**Prazosin** relaxes the urethra and is widely prescribed after a blockage. Two small placebo-controlled trials, at two doses, found no reduction in re-blocking [11, 12]. The retrospective harm signal is probably confounded: a "gritty" urethra or difficult catheterization predicted re-blocking [31], and those are the cats most likely to go home on the drug. Prazosin did shorten catheterization (median 24 vs 28 hours) [11].

**Diet after a stone.** Of 17 cats completing one prospective study, struvite stones dissolved in a median of 18 days in all but 4, whose stones proved to be calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate [8]. In a 37-cat randomized comparison of two dissolution foods, 32 cats' stones dissolved completely, in a mean of 13.0 or 27.0 days depending on the food; the 5 non-responders had urate or calcium oxalate stones, and no shrinkage by 2 weeks signaled a wrong diagnosis or a diet not followed [9]. In another study, 7 of 9 cats had complete dissolution by day 56, and one cat blocked when a shrinking stone entered the urethra [27]. An older study reported dissolution in 31 of 39 cats in an average of 30 days [35].

**Diet after cystitis.** In a randomized, masked trial in cats with non-obstructive FIC (25 analyzed), a therapeutic food did not significantly reduce the share of cats with at least one multi-sign flare (4 of 11 vs 9 of 14), but such flares fell from 5.4 to 0.7 episodes per 1,000 cat-days and fewer cats needed pain relief (4 of 11 vs 12 of 14) [36]. In a 31-cat cohort, a therapeutic urinary diet with calming ingredients was linked to fewer recurrences over 5 weeks (29.4% vs 78.6%; odds ratio 8.8, 95% CI 1.69 to 45.76), but owners chose the diet [37]. The 2025 systematic review found the strongest support for stress-reducing environmental changes and therapeutic urinary foods, especially with more moisture, and insufficient evidence for anti-inflammatory drugs, glycosaminoglycans, prazosin alone and short-term amitriptyline [26].

## What the evidence does not show

- **That wet food prevents blockages in pet cats.** Prevention evidence is mostly urine chemistry in 6 to 36 healthy research cats per study; the most direct clinical evidence is one older, non-randomized study [25].
- **That supersaturation tells the whole story.** In data from 78 healthy cats, calcium oxalate supersaturation did not differ significantly between urinary and ordinary foods (3.33 vs 3.71), yet calcium oxalate crystals were found in 11 urine samples on ordinary foods versus 1 on urinary foods [38].
- **That salt-boosted diets suit cats with heart or kidney disease.** Safety was shown only in healthy cats [5, 29].
- **That supplements work.** In a 21-cat cranberry extract trial, signs cleared in the supplemented group by day 60, but both groups improved by day 30 and urinalysis did not differ [39]. An herbal compound lowered struvite supersaturation in 6 healthy cats, with no clinical outcomes [40].
- **Anything about surgery or stress reduction on its own,** which Review 2 did not assess directly.

Our own limits: 31 of 46 studies in Review 1 and 16 of 29 in Review 2 were read only at abstract level, no formal risk-of-bias grading was applied, and 15 records in Review 2 remain unresolved.

## What this means for cat owners

- **A blocked cat is an emergency.** A cat, especially a male, that strains and passes little or no urine needs a veterinarian immediately, even at night. Do not wait to see if it passes. After unblocking, watch closely: re-blocking clusters in the first hours after catheter removal and the first weeks at home [11].
- **Get water in through the food.** Wet food is the best-supported option; adding water to dry food also helped in a small trial [2].
- **A fountain will not hurt**, and some cats prefer one bowl type, but do not rely on it to dilute urine [6].
- **Choose therapeutic, salt- or potassium-enhanced diets with your vet**, especially if your cat has heart or kidney problems.
- **Ask for follow-up X-rays during stone dissolution.** A stone that is not shrinking may not be struvite [9].
- **Ask about urine concentration.** For cats with calcium oxalate stones, one clinical review suggests a urine specific gravity below 1.025, based on the authors' experience [18].
- **Talk to your vet about medications.** Trials do not show that prazosin prevents re-blocking, and routine antibiotics were not linked to fewer re-blocks. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without your vet's advice.
- **Treat urinary supplements as unproven** until trials in affected cats show a benefit.

This report summarizes published research. It is not veterinary advice; talk to your vet about your cat.
